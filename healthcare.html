02 / RESEARCH & DISCOVERY

Looking Beyond the Appointment

I began this project by exploring how people communicate and coordinate care in healthcare settings. Through observations and an interview, I became increasingly interested in what happens when patients and families are left to manage care on their own.

WHERE I STARTED

Learning from Real-World Care

ICU Observation Notes / Photo
01 / VOLUNTEER OBSERVATION

Inside a Surgical ICU

While volunteering in a surgical ICU, I observed how nurses, physicians, and staff coordinated patient care. Even with digital tools and whiteboards, verbal communication remained essential for clarifying information and responsibilities.

This made me question how people without medical training navigate similarly complex information.

Interview Notes / Research Artifact
02 / QUALITATIVE INTERVIEW

Understanding Care at Home

I spoke with a retired nurse with homecare experience to better understand the everyday responsibilities involved in supporting patients outside clinical settings.

This shifted my attention toward family caregivers, who often manage health information alongside work and personal responsibilities.

WHAT THE RESEARCH REVEALED

Three Insights That Shaped My Thinking

01

Care Involves More Than One Person

Patients, caregivers, and providers all contribute to care. Family caregivers often help communicate and remember information while balancing their own responsibilities.

The challenge isn't only individual — it's shared.
02

Translation Doesn't Always Mean Understanding

Even with language support, unfamiliar medical terms and complex explanations can make it difficult for patients to fully participate in conversations about their care.

Accessibility goes beyond language alone.
03

Care Continues Between Visits

Symptoms change, questions arise, and details become difficult to remember. Patients and caregivers must manage this information long after an appointment ends.

Important information often gets lost over time.
REFRAMING THE PROBLEM

From communication during appointments to managing care between them.

I initially focused on what happens during medical conversations. But my exploration revealed another challenge: how much patients and families are expected to remember, organize, and communicate on their own.

I wanted to explore how care at home could feel less overwhelming, while helping patients stay involved in their own health — without creating another demanding tracking routine.

03 / UNDERSTANDING THE CARE JOURNEY

Understanding the Work Behind Care

To explore how patients and family caregivers manage care across different settings, I developed a research-informed scenario following an older patient undergoing cancer treatment and her daughter.

INTRODUCING THE PERSONAS

Two People, Different Responsibilities

THE PATIENT

Linda

Living with cancer and ongoing sleep problems

Linda sometimes struggles to describe her symptoms or understand unfamiliar medical terms. Although her daughter helps with appointments, she wants to stay involved in managing her own health.

NEEDS A simple way to express how she feels without always relying on someone else.
THE FAMILY CAREGIVER

Samantha

Balancing caregiving with work and everyday life

Samantha helps her mother prepare for appointments, communicate with providers, and remember health concerns. But she isn't a healthcare professional and can't always be there to track every detail.

NEEDS A clearer way to support her mother without having to remember and manage everything.

WHAT I TOOK AWAY

Much of the mental work involved in care happens outside the appointment.

This analysis helped me see an opportunity to reduce the information patients and caregivers must remember, while supporting a more active role for patients in their own care.

05 / INTRODUCING THE SOLUTION

A Simpler Way to Manage Care Between Visits

Capture, Organize & Share is a mobile experience designed to help patients record symptoms, review changes over time, and prepare for conversations with healthcare providers.

01 / RECORD SYMPTOMS Capture → 02 / REVIEW CHANGES Organize → 03 / PREPARE FOR VISITS Share
01 / CAPTURE

Record How You Feel, Without the Extra Effort

Tracking symptoms shouldn't feel like another chore. Patients can select a symptom, indicate whether they're feeling Better, Same, or Worse, and save an entry in just a few steps.

Optional text and voice inputs allow patients to add more context without requiring detailed medical descriptions.

For Linda, this means being able to record a difficult night of sleep on her own, without waiting for Samantha's help.

02 / ORGANIZE

See the Bigger Picture, Not Just Individual Symptoms

A single symptom entry may not tell the whole story. Over time, small changes can become difficult to remember.

The Health Timeline brings recorded information together through two levels: an at-a-glance overview of symptom changes and a chronological list of records that can be filtered by category.

Instead of relying on memory, Linda and Samantha can look back at the changes Linda has already recorded.

03 / SHARE

Make Important Conversations a Little Easier

Preparing for an appointment shouldn't mean piecing together weeks of information from memory.

By reviewing recorded concerns and symptom changes, patients and caregivers can identify what matters and prepare relevant information to discuss with their healthcare provider.

For Samantha, this means having a clearer picture of Linda's recent health. For Linda, it's an opportunity to communicate her own experiences.

DESIGNED AROUND EVERYDAY LIFE

The goal isn't to ask patients to track more. It's to make what matters easier to capture, find, and communicate.

06 / DESIGN DECISIONS & ITERATIONS

Designing for Less Effort, More Control

Throughout the design process, I focused on making health information easier to record, understand, and manage. These three decisions helped shape an experience that supports patients without adding unnecessary complexity.

DECISION 01

Simplifying Symptom Input

Making health tracking feel less like a task.

I wanted symptom recording to feel approachable, especially for older patients who may be unfamiliar with medical terminology or complicated interfaces.

Instead of requiring numerical severity ratings or lengthy descriptions, I designed a simple three-option system: Better, Same, or Worse. Optional text and voice inputs allow patients to provide more detail when needed.

Design Rationale

Reducing the number of decisions and making additional details optional helps keep symptom recording manageable.

The Tradeoff

Three options are easier to understand, but may not capture the level of detail needed in every medical situation.

DECISION 02

Making Health History Easier to Review

From individual entries to a clearer picture.

A long list of health records can make it difficult to recognize meaningful changes over time.

I refined the Health Timeline into two levels: an At a Glance overview that summarizes symptom changes, and All Records, a chronological history with category filters.

Design Rationale

Separating the overview from detailed records allows patients to scan recent changes while still accessing more complete information.

The Tradeoff

Too much information can overwhelm users, while oversimplification may hide important details. The two-level structure aims to balance both needs.

DECISION 03

Supporting Different Levels of Assistance

Independence doesn't mean doing everything alone.

Patients have different levels of confidence, language proficiency, and comfort with technology. I wanted to support patient independence without assuming everyone could set up the experience alone.

This led me to explore flexible onboarding, including language preferences, basic health information, and the possibility of setup assistance from family members or healthcare staff.

Design Rationale

Giving patients different ways to get started could make the experience more approachable while maintaining their involvement in care.

Further Consideration

Assisted setup raises important questions about privacy, consent, and access to patient information that require further exploration.

VISUAL DESIGN & COMPONENTS

Calm, Clear, and Consistent

I chose warm ivory and sage green to create a welcoming experience rather than a traditional clinical appearance.

I also developed reusable components, including symptom cards, button states, and status indicators, to maintain consistency across the interface.

07 / REFLECTION & NEXT STEPS

What I Learned Along the Way

This project challenged me to look beyond individual screens and understand the people, responsibilities, and everyday situations surrounding healthcare.

01

Research Can Change the Problem

I started by exploring communication during medical appointments, but my research led me to focus on the responsibilities patients and families manage between visits. It reminded me not to commit to a solution too early.

02

Simplicity Requires Tradeoffs

Making an experience easier to use isn't just about removing features. I had to consider what information patients really need, how much effort each interaction requires, and what might be lost through simplification.

LIMITATIONS & NEXT STEPS

What Still Needs to Be Explored

While the current design brings together my research and design exploration, it has not yet been validated through usability testing with target patients and family caregivers.

My next step would be to test whether the symptom recording flow is intuitive, whether the Health Timeline helps people review changes, and how assisted setup could work while respecting patient privacy and consent.

FINAL THOUGHT

Good design shouldn't ask more from people who already have enough to manage.

This project reinforced the kind of experiences I want to create: thoughtful, approachable tools that give people more control without adding unnecessary work to their lives.
