01 / RESEARCH & DISCOVERY

Looking Beyond the Appointment

I began this project by exploring how people communicate and coordinate care in healthcare settings. Through observations and an interview, I became increasingly interested in what happens when patients and families are left to manage care on their own.

WHERE I STARTED

Learning from Real-World Care

ICU Observation Notes / Photo
01 / VOLUNTEER OBSERVATION

Inside a Surgical ICU

While volunteering in a surgical ICU, I observed how nurses, physicians, and staff coordinated patient care. Even with digital tools and whiteboards, verbal communication remained essential for clarifying information and responsibilities.

This made me question how people without medical training navigate similarly complex information.

Interview Notes / Research Artifact
02 / QUALITATIVE INTERVIEW

Understanding Care at Home

I spoke with a retired nurse with homecare experience to better understand the everyday responsibilities involved in supporting patients outside clinical settings.

This shifted my attention toward family caregivers, who often manage health information alongside work and personal responsibilities.

WHAT THE RESEARCH REVEALED

Three Insights That Shaped My Thinking

01

Care Involves More Than One Person

Patients, caregivers, and providers all contribute to care. Family caregivers often help communicate and remember information while balancing their own responsibilities.

The challenge is shared among family members.
02

Translation Doesn't Always Mean Understanding

Even with language support, unfamiliar medical terms and complex explanations can make it difficult for patients to fully participate in conversations about their care.

Language itself is not the main problem.
03

Care Continues Between Visits

Symptoms change, questions arise, and details become difficult to remember. Patients and caregivers must manage this information long after an appointment ends.

Important information often gets lost over time.
REFRAMING THE PROBLEM

From communication during appointments to managing care between them.

I initially focused on what happens during medical appointment. But my research revealed another challenge: how much patients and families are expected to remember, organize, and communicate on their own.

I wanted to explore how care at home could feel less overwhelming, while helping patients stay involved in their own health, without creating another tracking routine.

02 / UNDERSTANDING THE CARE JOURNEY

Understanding the Work Behind Care

To explore how patients and family caregivers manage care across different settings, I developed a research-informed scenario following an older patient undergoing cancer treatment and her daughter.

INTRODUCING THE PERSONAS

Two People, Different Responsibilities

THE PATIENT

Linda

Living with cancer and ongoing sleep problems

Linda sometimes struggles to describe her symptoms or understand unfamiliar medical terms. Although her daughter helps with appointments, she wants to stay involved in managing her own health.

NEEDS A simple way to express how she feels without always relying on someone else.
THE FAMILY CAREGIVER

Samantha

Balancing caregiving with work and everyday life

Samantha helps her mother prepare for appointments, communicate with providers, and remember health concerns. But she isn't a healthcare professional and can't always be there to track every detail.

NEEDS A clearer way to support her mother without having to remember and manage everything.

NEGATIVE SCENARIO

A Doctor's Appointment Is Only One Part of the Story

I mapped a hypothetical follow-up appointment to explore how Linda's sleep problems create challenges before, during, and after the visit.

01 / BEFORE

Trying to Remember

Linda's sleep has been getting worse, but neither she nor Samantha remembers exactly when the changes began.

02 / DURING

Too Much to Process

Samantha tries to listen, understand medical explanations, support Linda, and remember questions, all at the same time.

03 / AFTER

Piecing Things Together

Back at home, they struggle to recall recommendations and understand what to monitor before the next appointment.

SEQUENCE MODEL

Mapping How Information Moves

I created a sequence model to examine how information and responsibilities move between patients, family caregivers, and healthcare providers throughout the care journey.

COGNITIVE TASK ANALYSIS

Breaking Down the Mental Work

To understand why these responsibilities felt demanding, I broke the scenario into 10 cognitive tasks and grouped them into three areas.

01 / BEFORE

Remembering & Preparing

Recalling symptom changes, collecting questions, and deciding what matters most before an appointment.

CHALLENGE Important details build up over time without an easy way to capture them.
02 / DURING

Communicating & Understanding

Listening to explanations, supporting the patient, processing unfamiliar information, and remembering questions.

CHALLENGE Multiple mental tasks compete for attention during conversations.
03 / AFTER

Tracking & Following Up

Remembering recommendations, reviewing next steps, and monitoring changes in everyday life.

CHALLENGE Patients and caregivers must carry information from visits into daily life.

WHAT I TOOK AWAY

Much of the cognitive load involved in care happens outside the appointment.

This analysis helped me see an opportunity to reduce the information patients and caregivers must remember, while supporting a more active role for patients in their own care.

03 / DEFINING & EXPLORING OPPORTUNITIES

From Research to Possibilities

After breaking down the cognitive work involved in caregiving, I began exploring how design could reduce the effort required to manage health information, both during appointments and at home.

HOW MIGHT WE

Turning Challenges into Questions

HMW 08 / DURING VISITS

How might we help Samantha keep track of recommendations, actions, and next steps during appointments without relying on memory alone?

Reducing the mental effort of listening, remembering, and supporting Linda during medical conversations.

HMW 10 / AT HOME

How might we help Linda and Samantha clearly understand what actions to take and what symptoms to monitor at home?

Making important health information easier to manage between appointments.

IDEATION

Exploring 19 Different Ideas

Rather than jumping straight into a solution, I brainstormed 19 ideas across both HMW questions, exploring different ways to support communication and everyday care.

09 IDEAS

During Appointments

Recommendation checklists Shared action boards Provider-confirmed notes

10 IDEAS

Between Appointments

Symptom check-ins Shared care plans Reminders Visualizations

CONCEPT EVALUATION

Comparing Support and Effort

I evaluated the ideas on a 1–5 scale using criteria tailored to each HMW question, considering user support, cognitive load, and provider effort.

What I found: Some ideas made it easier for patients to know what to do, but required more work from healthcare providers. Others helped patients to have more control, but didn't always make the next steps clear.

DESIGN SPACE

Comparing Different Directions

I mapped the ideas onto two dimensions: patient sense of control and the amount of useful information available to providers between appointments.

I became particularly interested in approaches that could give patients more ownership of their health while reducing their reliance on family caregivers. This led me to focus on three simple actions: capturing symptoms, organizing health information, and sharing what matters.

04 / INTRODUCING THE SOLUTION

A Simpler Way to Manage Care Between Visits

01 / RECORD SYMPTOMS Capture → 02 / REVIEW CHANGES Organize → 03 / PREPARE FOR VISITS Share
WELCOME & ONBOARDING

Getting Started, at Your Own Pace

Patients can choose their preferred language, set up their health profile, and ask someone they trust for help when needed. Optional information can be skipped and added later.

01 / CAPTURE

Record How You Feel, Without the Extra Effort

Tracking symptoms shouldn't feel like another chore. Patients can select a symptom, indicate whether they're feeling Better, Same, or Worse, and save an entry in just a few steps.

Optional text and voice inputs allow patients to add more context without requiring detailed medical descriptions.

For Linda, this means being able to record a difficult night of sleep on her own, without waiting for Samantha's help.

A CLOSER LOOK

Inside the Capture Flow

02 / ORGANIZE

See the Bigger Picture, Not Just Individual Symptoms

A single symptom entry may not tell the whole story. Over time, small changes can become difficult to remember.

The Health Timeline gives patients a quick overview of their health concerns. Selecting a symptom opens its history, where they can review recent changes and previous records in chronological order.

Instead of relying on memory, Linda and Samantha can look back at the changes Linda has recorded.

03 / SHARE

Make Important Conversations a Little Easier

Preparing for an appointment shouldn't mean piecing together weeks of information from memory.

By reviewing recorded concerns and symptom changes, patients and caregivers can identify what matters and prepare relevant information to discuss with their healthcare provider.

For Samantha, this means having a clearer picture of Linda's recent health. For Linda, it's an opportunity to communicate her own experiences.

↳ WHAT GUIDED THE DESIGN

The goal isn't to ask patients to track more. It's to make what matters easier to capture, find, and communicate.

This principle shaped three core experiences: capturing symptom changes with less effort, organizing health information over time, and preparing what matters for care conversations.

05 / DESIGN DECISIONS & ITERATIONS

Designing for Less Effort, More Control

Throughout the design process, I focused on making health information easier to record, understand, and manage. These three decisions helped shape an experience that supports patients without adding unnecessary complexity.

DESIGN NOTE 01

Making Symptom Recording Feel Manageable

Patients may struggle to describe how they feel using medical terminology or numerical ratings.

Why I chose this approach

Better, Same, and Worse provide a quick way to describe changes. Optional text and voice inputs allow patients to add context without making detailed descriptions mandatory.

THE TRADEOFF ↗

Three options keep recording simple, but cannot capture every detail. Additional notes provide flexibility when more context is needed.

DESIGN NOTE 02

Making Health History Easier to Understand

Reviewing a long list of individual records can make it difficult to notice recent changes.

Why I chose this approach

The Health Timeline provides two levels of information: an At a Glance summary for a quick overview, followed by dated records for patients who need more detail.

THE TRADEOFF ↗

Summaries make information easier to scan, but may leave out important context. Detailed records remain available below.

DESIGN NOTE 03

Supporting Independence Without Assuming It

Patients have different levels of confidence, language proficiency, and comfort with technology.

Why I chose this approach

Patients can choose their preferred language, ask someone they trust for help, and skip optional health information until later.

FURTHER CONSIDERATION ↗

Assisted setup introduces questions about patient privacy, consent, and access to personal health information that still need further exploration.

VISUAL DESIGN & COMPONENTS

Calm, Clear, and Consistent

I chose warm ivory and sage green to create a welcoming experience rather than a traditional clinical appearance.

I also developed reusable components, including symptom cards, button states, and status indicators, to maintain consistency across the interface.

06 / REFLECTION & NEXT STEPS

What I Learned Along the Way

This project challenged me to look beyond individual screens and understand the people, responsibilities, and everyday situations surrounding healthcare.

01

Research Can Change the Problem

I started by exploring communication during medical appointments, but my research led me to focus on the responsibilities patients and families manage between visits. It reminded me not to commit to a solution too early.

02

Simplicity Requires Tradeoffs

Making an experience easier to use isn't just about removing features. I had to consider what information patients really need, how much effort each interaction requires, and what might be lost through simplification.

LIMITATIONS & NEXT STEPS

What Still Needs to Be Explored

While the current design brings together my research and design exploration, it has not yet been validated through usability testing with target patients and family caregivers.

  1. Symptom Recording Is the Better / Same / Worse flow intuitive for older patients?
  2. Health Timeline Can patients and caregivers easily review changes and find previous records?
  3. Assisted Setup How can family members or staff help while respecting patient privacy and consent?
FINAL THOUGHT

Good design shouldn't ask more from people who already have enough to manage.

This project reinforced the kind of experiences I want to create: thoughtful, approachable tools that give people more control without adding unnecessary work to their lives.
